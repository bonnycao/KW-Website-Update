Ewill Rabi’s Story

My name is Ewill Rabi born in Karikaria, Sudan, where my earliest memories were filled with stories told under the shade of acacia trees. Conflict soon forced my family to flee, and I found refuge in Kakuma Refugee Camp in Kenya. Life in Kakuma was harsh, long queues for water, crowded shelters, and limited opportunities but I refused to let these circumstances define me.

At school, I discovered a small computer center run by volunteers. The first time I touched a keyboard, I felt a new world open before me. Though access was limited, I spent hours learning basic typing, exploring offline lessons, and asking endless questions. Digital literacy became my passion and hope.

As I grew older, I began teaching younger children what I had learned, sharing knowledge with patience and determination. I realized that technology could empower my community, giving them skills to connect, learn, and grow despite displacement.

Driven by this vision, I set my sights on becoming a leader in program management, especially in digital literacy initiatives. I dream of designing programs that equip refugees with digital skills, helping them build better futures. From Karikaria to Kakuma, my journey is one of resilience, purpose, and the power of knowledge.

My story shows that even in the hardest conditions, determination and access to knowledge can shape a powerful future.

Leonce Mucho’s Story

My name is Leonce Mucho, I have stayed in Kakuma Refugee Camp for over 10 years to date, experiencing numerous challenges due to the environmental conditions, one of them being hot weather. And as a matter of fact in general life in kakuma is too hard. Actually I completed both primary school and high school in the camp. However, I never gave up on learning, even when it was difficult.

Life in Kakuma gave me many challenges. And truly I suffered from financial instability. When I was in high school I struggled a lot in ensuring I stabilize my studies regardless of how hard it was even to get revision materials.

Second, I had no access to digital tools, which could help me do more research during my studies. There was no laptop or internet for me to learn computer skills, and as we know many good jobs today need these skills, but I could not get them.

Third, after high school, I could not find a suitable job that could at least have me some stipends for my basic needs. I applied many times, but the institutions and companies always told me, I should have an experience. But how can I get experience if no one gives me a chance to work? I felt stuck and worried about my future.

This year, I joined the KeelMaster program through VDPA Community Base Organization after I heard that Keelworks helps young people who have no jobs and its main purpose is to journey with unemployed youth and help them break the barrier to sustainable work and growth.

The program also helps undergraduates and graduates who lack work experience. Keelworks gives us valuable employment coaching so that we can find real jobs.

Through Keelworks, my life was again filled with hope that I will no longer have a job and keep preparing my mind for further studies. Currently I'm still under keelMaster training which is done on a weekly bases, and gives me a chance to interact with others in the same program through discussions and sessions being equipped with problem solving skills, critical thinking skills, assertiveness and modes of studying.

The coaches at Keelworks made me now understand what companies want from workers and I'm no longer afraid to apply for jobs.

Indeed, Kakuma taught me to be strong and patient, the challenges were many, but they did not break me. Keelworks are giving me skills and hope. My dream is to get a sustainable job in technology. Then I will make a transformation to other youth in Kakuma who feel stuck like I did. I want to show them that with the right support, we can all grow and succeed.

I am grateful to Keelworks for seeing my potential and believing in me. My story is and will still be written, but now I hold the pen with confidence and hope, truly, kakuma tested me, but the hope ignited by keelworks will carry me to my future.

Samaidi Fiston Bin Chris's Story

My name is Samaidi Fiston Binchris. I was born in the Democratic Republic of Congo, in Uvira, South Kivu Province—an area rich in natural resources but deeply affected by conflict and instability.

From an early age, life was marked by hardship. Growing up in a conflict-affected environment meant living in constant uncertainty and fear. Access to basic needs, especially education, was limited. Many children, including myself, struggled to maintain consistent schooling, and as a result, many dreams were interrupted before they could fully develop.

Despite these challenges, I developed a strong passion for learning during my secondary education. I was particularly interested in Monitoring and Evaluation, a field that focuses on assessing impact, improving programs, and creating meaningful change in communities. My dream was to one day attend university and specialize in this field, so that I could contribute to development work and help improve the lives of others.

In addition to my academic ambitions, I also aspired to become an entrepreneur. I envisioned building my own production company, an initiative that could create opportunities, provide employment, and contribute to economic growth within my community.

However, these dreams have faced significant obstacles. Due to ongoing insecurity, I was forced to leave my country and became a refugee. Life in the refugee camp presents new and complex challenges. The environment is difficult, and opportunities are limited. One of the major barriers I face is language. As a French speaker, I often find myself at a disadvantage in systems that prioritize English. Accessing education requires either starting again from primary level or first completing English language training, which delays progress and adds another layer of difficulty.

These realities have made my goals seem distant and, at times, overwhelming. Nevertheless, I have not given up. I remain determined to overcome these barriers, improve my language skills, and continue pursuing my education. I believe that with the right support and opportunities, I can still achieve my dreams.

My journey is not only about personal success, but also about making a difference. I want to use my knowledge in Monitoring and Evaluation to support organizations, improve programs, and bring positive change to vulnerable communities. At the same time, I hope to one day realize my entrepreneurial vision and contribute to economic empowerment.

Although my path has been filled with challenges, I carry resilience, determination, and hope. I believe that my story is still being written, and that the future holds opportunities beyond the limitations I face today.

This is my story—a story of struggle, ambition, and the determination to rise above circumstances.

My Dreams and Vision: My biggest dream is to become a Monitoring and Evaluation (M&E) specialist. When I was in high school, we witnessed the theft of many projects carried out in our school to help people. I was shocked that many projects were not successful because there was no real evaluation. I want to be someone who uses statistics and evidence to ensure that every penny spent brings real change to communities in need. That is my main professional goal.

In addition, I have another dream on the entrepreneurial side. I want to start my own production company. My vision here is not just to make a profit, but to create jobs for my fellow youth and provide opportunities for others to rise economically. I believe that entrepreneurship is an important way to bring peace and sustainable development.

My ultimate goal is to see that my journey, despite all the obstacles of refugees and language, will come to be a help to others. I want to prove that where I came from or my current situation does not determine my destiny.

Sadik Mohamed’s Story

My name is Sadik Mohamed, and I was born in Saraf Jamus Payam, a small Sudanese village. There, life was easy but difficult. For survival, we relied on farming and cattle. Conflict, however, interfered with our peace, safety, and education. I had to escape, traveling great distances on foot before arriving at the Kakuma Refugee Camp in Kenya.

Kakuma was a difficult place to live. Food was scarce, opportunities were few, and the future was uncertain. I enrolled in the camp's elementary and secondary schools as soon as I got there because I was determined to finish my education in spite of the difficulties. I worked as a teacher at Kalobeyei Morning Star Primary and Junior School for two years after graduating from high school. I also participated in various initiatives to gain counseling skills. Knowing that education and technology could lead to opportunities, I also enrolled in the Jesuit Worldwide Learning program (JWL) to study computer skills and learning facilitation.

Life in Kakuma was not easy. There was limited food, few opportunities, and an uncertain future. When I arrived, I joined primary and secondary school within the camp, determined to continue my education despite the challenges. After completing high school, I joined Kalobeyei Morning Star primary and Junior school as a teacher for two years also joined different initiatives to learn counseling skills, I also joined Jesuit world wide learning program (JWL) to study learning facilitator, and computer skills knowing that education and technology could open doors for me.

Life continued to be challenging despite these efforts, but I persisted in pursuing my goals. My life changed when I enrolled in the KeelMaster program. It changed my perspective by giving me knowledge, self-assurance, and useful skills. I found new opportunities and developed the bravery to follow my dreams thanks to this program.

I now see past my difficulties with optimism and resolve. I now believe in learning, growth, and resilience as a result of my journey.
